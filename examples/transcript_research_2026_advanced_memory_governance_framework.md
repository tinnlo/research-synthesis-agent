### Final Research Report

# Advanced Memory Governance in 2026: Context Cartography and Related Frameworks

## Executive Summary

By 2026, “memory” for LLM-based agents has become a distinct engineering and research discipline rather than an afterthought bolted onto prompts. Systems now treat memory as a governed, multi-layer architecture spanning working context, episodic logs, semantic knowledge, and cold archives, with explicit policies for what to write, keep, compress, or forget. Benchmarks like LoCoMo, MemBench, MemoryAgentBench, and MemoryArena, alongside large surveys, show that naive “append the history into the prompt” approaches hit hard limits in task success, cost, and robustness, especially for long-horizon and multi-session agents.[1][5][20][23]

Within this landscape, **Context Cartography** (2026) stands out as the first fully formal governance-layer framework that explicitly models *contextual space* and *epistemic zones* and defines operators for moving information between them.[6][16][14] It complements OS-inspired hierarchical memory systems such as MemGPT/Letta, production frameworks like Mem0, and graph-based architectures like GAM and context graphs.[2][3][11][8] A convergent pattern is emerging: agents maintain a small “visible field” for active reasoning, a richer but curated “gray fog” of stored memory, and mechanisms to reach into the “black fog” of external knowledge, all governed by explicit rules or learned controllers. However, open problems remain around learned governance, multi-agent memory, security/privacy, and normative questions of what agents *ought* to remember or forget.[5][21][22][26]

## 1. Conceptual Foundations of Advanced Memory Governance

### 1.1 From “conversation history” to governed memory systems

Between 2023 and 2026, research and practice shifted from treating memory as raw conversation history stuffed into an LLM context window to treating it as a governed module with its own abstractions, metrics, and tooling.[1][3][5][26] Surveys and state-of-practice reports emphasize:

- **Multiple memory realizations**: parametric (weights), contextual (tokens in the prompt), and external memory (databases, logs, graphs, tools).[5][26]  
- **Functional roles**: working/short-term context, episodic memory of events, semantic knowledge, and sometimes procedural/skill memory.[4][5][23]  
- **Lifecycle view**: write → manage → read loops, including consolidation, conflict resolution, and forgetting.[5][22][26]

The 2026 survey *Memory for Autonomous LLM Agents* explicitly frames memory as a modular component with three main architectural patterns: monolithic context, context+retrieval, and tiered memory with learned control.[5][23] Parallel work on “memory in LLMs” adds a taxonomy of memory types and a governance focus on updating and forgetting.[26]

### 1.2 Key governance concepts and terminology

Across research and engineering blogs, several shared concepts have crystallized:

- **Context engineering / context management** – deciding what enters the context window, how it is structured, and how it is refreshed.[13][15]  
- **Memory routing / gating** – rules or learned policies that determine which memories are retrieved, promoted to working context, or evicted.[5][11][13][15]  
- **Retrieval governance** – controlling *when* to retrieve, from *where*, and *how much*, often under token and latency budgets.[5][13][15][20]  
- **Curation and consolidation** – deduplicating, resolving contradictions, summarizing, and turning episodic fragments into stable semantic knowledge.[1][3][11][20][22]  
- **Forgetting / garbage collection** – explicit mechanisms (decay, access-based pruning, policy-driven deletion) to avoid bloat and stale or unsafe memories.[13][20][22][26]  
- **Security and mnemonic sovereignty** – governing who can write/read memory, how poisoning and leakage are prevented, and how user control over their data is maintained.[21][22]

Advanced memory governance frameworks are those that make these concerns *first-class*, usually with explicit formalisms for zones/layers, transition policies, and failure modes.

## 2. The Context Cartography Framework

### 2.1 Authors, scope, and positioning

**Context Cartography: Toward Structured Governance of Contextual Space in Large Language Model Systems** (arXiv:2603.20578, March 2026) proposes a formal governance-layer framework for LLM-based systems.[6][16] It is explicitly positioned as:

- A **general context-governance framework**: not tied to a specific agent architecture, but applicable across single- and multi-agent systems, RAG pipelines, and memory-augmented agents.[6][16]  
- A **governance-layer competitor** to frameworks like MemOS and “memory as metabolism,” focusing on zones, operators, and failure modes rather than specific data structures.[14][6]  
- A framework with **mechanically verified components**: parts of the operator semantics are formalized in Lean 4, signaling a push toward mathematically grounded governance.[14][6]

### 2.2 Epistemic zones and contextual universe

Context Cartography introduces a precise partition of the **contextual universe** \( U \) at time \( t \):[6]

- **Visible Field \( V_t \)** – the *active reasoning surface*: tokens currently in the LLM context window, plus immediate scratchpad.  
- **Gray Fog \( G_t \)** – *stored memory*: information previously observed and retained in external structures (logs, vector stores, graphs, DBs).  
- **Black Fog \( B_t \)** – the *unobserved frontier*: information potentially relevant but not yet observed or stored (e.g., web, tools, future user inputs).

Formally, \( U = B_t \cup G_t \cup V_t \) with disjoint zones.[6] This epistemic partition clarifies:

- What it means to “retrieve” (a transition from \( B \) or \( G \) into \( V \)).  
- Why naive concatenation of history is flawed: it collapses \( G \) and \( V \) and ignores temporal and reliability differences.[6][13][15]  
- How to reason about **governance failures** at the level of transitions between zones.

### 2.3 Cartographic operators and pipelines

The framework defines **seven cartographic operators** (summarized in its Table 3) that act on zones and transitions.[6] While the paper’s notation is detailed, conceptually they fall into:

- **Projection operators (π)** – move or project content between zones, especially into \( V \) (e.g., π⁺: promotion/retrieval into visible field; π⁻: compaction/eviction from \( V \) back to \( G \)).[6]  
- **Internal maintenance operators (ϕ, α, λ, etc.)** – operate within \( G \): reorganizing, abstracting, or pruning stored memory (e.g., clustering, summarizing, graph restructuring).[6][3]  
- **Filtering and alignment operators** – ensure that what enters \( V \) is relevant, non-contradictory, and appropriately layered (e.g., core vs peripheral context).[6][14]

An **inbound pipeline** governs how new observations or tool outputs are incorporated into \( G \) (or discarded), while an **outbound pipeline** governs how \( G \) and \( B \) are sampled and projected into \( V \) each turn.[6] A minimal reference architecture includes:

- A **structured \( G \)** (e.g., vector DB + graph + logs).  
- A **context assembler** that uses the operators to build \( V \) at each step.  
- A **decision layer at the ρ/σ boundary** that routes knowledge gaps to either tool invocation (probing \( B \)) or memory recall (probing \( G \)) based on epistemic state.[6]

### 2.4 Failure modes and governance laws

Context Cartography reinterprets familiar issues as **transition-level failures**:[6]

- **Gray Fog / memory failures**:  
  - *Drift and bloat*: chronological logs in \( G \) yield unstructured, noisy retrieval that overwhelms \( V \).[6][13]  
  - *Context collapse*: monolithic rewriting/summarization erodes nuanced, accumulated knowledge.[6]  
- **Visible Field failures**:  
  - *Overload and dilution*: too many tokens in \( V \) reduce effective capacity; the model attends poorly to key information.[6][15]  
  - *Brevity bias*: optimization toward short, generic outputs when \( V \) is overloaded or poorly structured.[6]

The paper argues that **effective capacity is far below nominal context length**; without active filtering and structuring, agents effectively use only a fraction of available tokens.[6][15] It proposes **“cartographic laws”**—normative constraints on how \( V, G, B \) should be managed—to avoid these failure modes, and evaluates the framework via **operator ablation** (turning off specific operators and measuring degradation).[6][14]

### 2.5 Position relative to other systems

Context Cartography explicitly maps to existing systems:[6]

- **RAG**: seen as a partial π⁺ from \( B \) to \( V \), mainly concerned with *selection* from a pre-indexed corpus, not with how retrieved content is layered or compressed within \( V \).[6]  
- **MemGPT / Letta**: OS-inspired tiered memory; interpreted as particular instantiations of \( G \) with fixed π⁺/π⁻ policies and background maintenance (sleep-time consolidation).[6][3]  
- **MemOS**: another governance-layer approach with typed memory containers; Context Cartography positions itself as more general and zone/transition-centric.[6][14]  
- **Graph-based memory (Zep, Cognee, GAM)**: seen as specific structural choices for \( G \) plus particular internal operators (e.g., graph consolidation).[3][11]

In short, Context Cartography provides **theoretical scaffolding** into which many existing engineering patterns can be embedded.

## 3. Other Advanced Memory Governance Frameworks (2023–2026)

### 3.1 OS-inspired hierarchical memory: MemGPT / Letta and MemOS

**MemGPT / Letta (2024–2025)** treat LLM context as a kind of **virtual memory** managed by an OS-like controller.[3][6]

- **Layers/zones**:  
  - *Core memory*: always-present, high-resolution context projected into \( V \).  
  - *Recall memory*: overflow tier; evicted content is summarized and archived.  
  - *Archival store*: long-term, compressed memory (often vector-based).[3][6]
- **Policies**:  
  - Fixed π⁺: core memory is persistently projected into \( V \).  
  - π⁻ compaction: when \( V \) is full, content is condensed and moved to recall/archival tiers.  
  - Background “sleep-time” processes apply consolidation operators within \( G \) (e.g., clustering, summarization).[6]
- **Goals**: maintain a small, stable working set while still allowing access to deep history.

**MemOS (circa 2025)** extends this into a **memory operating system** with *typed containers* (“Mem-Cubes”), each with its own update and access policies.[6][14] It provides a governance layer but is more implementation-specific than Context Cartography.

### 3.2 Production frameworks: Mem0, LangMem, Zep, Cognee

Industry-facing frameworks have converged on similar ideas, with varying emphases:[1][2][3]

- **Mem0** (ECAI 2025, widely adopted by 2026):  
  - **Three-tier scopes**: user, session, and agent memory.[1][3]  
  - **Hybrid backing store**: vector search + graph relationships + key–value lookup.[3]  
  - **Governance features**:  
    - Self-editing to resolve conflicting facts instead of appending duplicates, keeping memory lean and reducing contradiction.[1][3]  
    - Curation pipelines that deduplicate and reconcile records over time.  
- **LangMem (LangChain ecosystem)**: integrates with LangGraph; focuses on easy integration and standard patterns for retrieval, summarization, and history management.[3]  
- **Zep**: graph-native temporal architecture (“Graphiti”) optimized for **temporal reasoning and event sequencing**; emphasizes **temporal obsolescence handling** and retrieval over time-aware graphs.[3][22]  
- **Cognee**: open-source **knowledge graph layer** for agents; organizes content as entities and relations, enabling **graph-based retrieval** and consolidation into a structured semantic memory.[2][3]

These frameworks implement practical **governance policies**: size limits per scope, conflict resolution, summarization of old sessions, and sometimes user-level controls over what is persisted.[1][2][3]

### 3.3 Hierarchical graph memory: GAM

**GAM (Hierarchical Graph-based Agentic Memory)** proposes a **Hierarchical Graph Memory Architecture** to address **memory contamination** and the stability–plasticity tradeoff.[11]

- **Zones**:  
  - *Global Topic Associative Network*: stable semantic graph capturing long-term knowledge.  
  - *Local Event Progression Graphs*: per-episode structures capturing fine-grained, rapidly changing context.[11]
- **Governance mechanisms**:  
  - **Write isolation**: new, noisy observations go into local episodic graphs, not directly into the global store.  
  - **State-based memory consolidation**: the system switches between an **Episodic Buffering State** and a **Semantic Consolidation State**; global memory is updated only when episodes reach semantically complete boundaries, detected via semantic divergence.[11]
- **Benefits**:  
  - Reduces contamination of global memory by transient or erroneous details.  
  - Provides a principled consolidation policy rather than arbitrary triggers.

GAM is conceptually close to Context Cartography’s distinction between active field vs gray fog, but instantiates it with explicit graph structures and state machines.

### 3.4 Multi-agent memory architectures

**Sterlites’ multi-agent memory system** (RDxClaw agent) frames memory as a **data movement** problem with a three-layer hierarchy:[9]

- **I/O layer** – ingestion of raw signals (documents, events, messages).  
- **Cache layer** – immediate reasoning context; akin to \( V \).  
- **Memory layer** – persistent knowledge; akin to \( G \).[9]

Governance concerns include:

- Coordinating memory across multiple agents so that teams “don’t lose their train of thought” mid-reasoning.[9]  
- Avoiding a “memory wall” where agents must re-read entire history to act, by aggressively caching and routing only relevant context.[9]

The 2026 memory survey notes that **multi-agent memory governance** is still immature: current systems mostly share logs or document stores, and open questions include access control, consensus for concurrent writes, and cross-agent knowledge transfer.[5][23]

### 3.5 Context graphs and enterprise context management

Beyond “agent memory” per se, there are **context governance** frameworks for enterprise data and knowledge:

- **Context Graphs** (Masood, 2025–2026): propose **graph-based governed context substrates** for LLMs and agents.[8]  
  - Combine **graph DB + vector search + rules/ACL + observability** as a hybrid stack.[8]  
  - Distinguish **GraphRAG** (a retrieval technique) from **context graphs** (the governed substrate from which retrieval operates).[8]  
  - Emphasize provenance, access control, and policy-driven retrieval in line with NIST-style governance frameworks.[8][10]
- **DataHub’s context management**: frames **technical, operational, and business context** as governed assets.[10]  
  - Encourages mapping where authoritative context lives, enforcing provenance and audit trails, and aligning with NIST AI RMF and OWASP Top 10 for LLM apps.[10]  
  - Treats context access as a policy-governed operation, not just ad hoc retrieval.

These systems extend memory governance into broader **organizational governance**, linking agent context to compliance, provenance, and risk management.

## 4. How Frameworks Model Zones, Movement, and Forgetting

### 4.1 Memory layers and zones

Across frameworks, there is strong convergence on **multi-layer memory**:

- **Working / visible field**: current context window, scratchpad, and immediate tool outputs.[5][6][15]  
- **Episodic memory**: event-level logs with temporal/spatial/causal metadata.[4][5][11]  
- **Semantic / global memory**: consolidated, de-duplicated knowledge (facts, preferences, rules), often in vector stores or graphs.[3][11][20][26]  
- **Cold archives**: low-cost storage for rarely accessed data (e.g., logs, blob stores).[5][1]

Context Cartography’s \( V, G, B \) abstraction serves as a **unifying lens**: OS-style tiers, graph memory, and enterprise context graphs can all be seen as particular structuring of \( G \) and its interfaces to \( V \) and \( B \).[6][3][8][11]

### 4.2 Policies for moving information between zones

Different systems implement different **movement policies**:

- **Heuristic / rule-based policies**:  
  - MemGPT/Letta: fixed rules for when to compact/evict from core context to recall/archival tiers.[6][3]  
  - Context engineering guides: truncation, summarization, and refresh triggers based on token counts, recency, and relevance heuristics.[13][15]  
  - Episodic memory systems: write a new memory entry when events meet “meaningfulness” thresholds (e.g., user-specific, outcome-changing).[4]
- **Learned controllers**:  
  - AgeMem, AtomMem, and related 2025–2026 work treat **memory control as a learnable policy**, using reinforcement learning or preference optimization to decide what to store, abstract, or retrieve.[5][22]  
  - Benchmarks like MemoryArena and LIGHT evaluate retrieval and consolidation strategies across long dialogs, showing gains when retrieval and working memory are coordinated.[20]
- **State-based consolidation**:  
  - GAM’s **Episodic Buffering vs Semantic Consolidation States** ensure that global memory is only updated at semantically coherent boundaries, detected via semantic divergence.[11]  
- **Governance-driven routing**:  
  - Context Cartography’s decision layer at the ρ/σ boundary routes gaps either to tools (probing \( B \)) or to memory recall from \( G \), based on epistemic state and operator-defined policies.[6]  
  - Enterprise context management frameworks gate access to context based on provenance, access control lists, and risk policies.[8][10]

### 4.3 Preventing context bloat, drift, and hallucinations

Common strategies to prevent **context bloat** and **hallucination amplification** include:

- **Summarization and condensation**:  
  - Summarizing long histories into compact state representations or narrative episodes.[13][15][20]  
  - Mem0’s self-editing and de-duplication keep memory lean and reduce contradictory retrieval.[1][3]
- **Selective retrieval and gating**:  
  - Treat retrieval as a **budgeted operation**, triggered based on uncertainty or entropy (e.g., L-RAG’s entropy-based lazy loading).[7][13][15]  
  - LIGHT and similar methods coordinate retrieval with working memory and noise filtering, showing large gains in summarization and multi-hop reasoning tasks.[20]
- **Write isolation and contamination control**:  
  - GAM separates local episodic buffers from global semantic graphs, updating the latter only after consolidation, mitigating memory contamination and self-reinforcing errors.[11]  
  - SSGM and security surveys emphasize **write filtering / firewalls** to block malicious or low-quality writes into long-term stores.[21][22]
- **Context structuring**:  
  - Treating the context window as layered (system instructions, static state, dynamic history, retrieved knowledge) rather than a flat token stream.[15][6]  
  - Context Cartography’s laws aim to keep \( V \) small, structured, and high-signal, reducing brevity bias and overload.[6][13]

These approaches reduce the risk that early mistakes or poisoned records dominate future reasoning, a problem highlighted in long-term benchmarks and security analyses.[20][21][22]

### 4.4 Forgetting, garbage collection, and lifecycle governance

Forgetting is increasingly treated as a **governed lifecycle process** rather than ad hoc truncation:

- **Access-based garbage collection**:  
  - Evict or compress memories that are infrequently accessed, merging overlapping records and deleting low-value entries.[13][22]  
- **Temporal decay**:  
  - Use decay functions (e.g., Weibull decay) to down-weight or retire stale memories, especially for personalization and time-sensitive knowledge.[22]  
- **Conflict-aware consolidation**:  
  - Resolve contradictions through explicit curation, sometimes preserving provenance and minority views rather than overwriting.[1][3][14]  
- **User and policy-driven deletion**:  
  - Security and governance frameworks stress **user rights to erasure**, **dynamic ACLs**, and policy-based retention limits.[21][22][10]

Surveys on LLM memory emphasize that **update and forgetting governance** is as important as retrieval quality, especially for safety, privacy, and long-term reliability.[21][22][26]

## 5. Evaluation, Benchmarks, and Empirical Findings

### 5.1 Benchmarks for long-term and agentic memory

From 2023–2026, a suite of benchmarks emerged to test memory in realistic settings:[5][20][23]

- **LoCoMo (2024)** – multi-session, multi-turn benchmark with long dialogues (~9–16k tokens) showing humans still far ahead of agents.[5][20]  
- **MemBench (ACL 2025)** – separates **factual vs reflective memory**, evaluating how agents recall facts vs learned reflections.[5][20]  
- **MemoryAgentBench (2025)** – tests four cognitive competencies (e.g., temporal reasoning, preference following); no current system masters all four.[5]  
- **MemoryArena (2026)** – multi-session, multi-turn, agentic tasks; couples memory with action and planning.[5][20]  
- **MemoryCode and newer long-memory benchmarks (ICLR 2026)** – scale to 10M tokens across diverse topics and introduce tasks like contradiction resolution and event ordering.[20]

These benchmarks typically report:

- **Task effectiveness**: success rate, factual correctness, plan completion.[5][20]  
- **Memory quality**: precision/recall of retrieved records, contradiction rate, staleness distribution, coverage of task-relevant facts.[5][20]  
- **Efficiency**: latency per memory operation, prompt tokens consumed by memory content, retrieval calls per step, storage growth.[5][20]  
- **Robustness and safety**: susceptibility to memory poisoning, drift, and contamination.[21][22]

### 5.2 Empirical benefits of advanced governance

While many governance frameworks are still early, several empirical patterns are visible:

- **Tiered memory with learned control** (e.g., AgeMem, LIGHT-style methods) shows **substantial gains** on long-memory benchmarks:  
  - LIGHT reports +160.6% in summarization, +27.2% in multi-hop reasoning, +76.5% in preference following, and strong improvements in temporal reasoning and information extraction vs baselines.[20]  
- **Self-editing and conflict-aware memory** (e.g., Mem0) reduce contradiction rate and improve retrieval precision, though detailed numbers are often proprietary.[1][3]  
- **Graph-based architectures** (Zep’s Graphiti, GAM) improve temporal reasoning and robustness to contamination by structuring memory as graphs and enforcing consolidation policies.[3][11][22]  
- **Context engineering practices** (structured prompts, gating, summarization) consistently reduce token cost and improve downstream accuracy and stability in production agents.[13][15][1]

However, surveys emphasize that **no architecture is dominant yet**: tiered, learned-control systems offer the most headroom but demand significant engineering and training resources, while context+retrieval patterns remain the workhorse in practice.[5][23]

## 6. Critical Perspectives, Limitations, and Open Problems

### 6.1 Brittleness, complexity, and learned vs rule-based control

Critiques focus on:

- **Heuristic brittleness**: rule-based truncation, summarization, or retrieval triggers often fail under distribution shift; small changes in workloads can break carefully tuned policies.[13][15][5]  
- **Engineering complexity**: tiered memory with learned controllers (MemGPT-style, AgeMem, AtomMem) is powerful but expensive to design, train, and operate.[5][22][23]  
- **Evaluation gaps**: Context Cartography evaluates via operator ablation, and “Memory as Metabolism” targets specific failure modes, but there is no widely accepted standard for governance-layer evaluation.[6][14][21]

There is an active debate between **rule-based governance** (transparent but brittle) and **learned governance** (adaptive but opaque and harder to verify).[5][22][26]

### 6.2 Multi-agent scaling and coherence

Multi-agent systems introduce new governance challenges:

- **Cross-agent projection incoherence**: when agents exchange summaries, each implicitly applies π⁺ across agent boundaries; incompatible schemas can yield well-formed but semantically distorted content in the receiver’s \( V \).[6]  
- **Theory-of-mind limitations**: failure analyses show agents often mis-model each other’s informational needs; adding explicit ToM mechanisms improves multi-agent reasoning but increases complexity.[6][5]  
- **Shared memory governance**: frameworks like Collab.Mem and MAMA (discussed in SSGM and security surveys) explore distributed consensus, dynamic ACLs, and topology-aware privacy, but this area is still nascent.[21][22]

The 2026 memory survey explicitly flags **multi-agent memory governance** as an open frontier.[5][23]

### 6.3 Security, privacy, and mnemonic sovereignty

Security-focused surveys and frameworks highlight serious risks:[21][22]

- **Memory & context poisoning**: adversaries inject malicious instructions or biased content into long-term stores; attacks like InjecMEM show high success rates in MemoryOS-style systems.[21]  
- **Privacy leakage**: unauthorized cross-session or cross-user retrieval; multi-tenant agents can leak private information if ACLs and provenance are weak.[21][22]  
- **Lack of governance in practice**: an IBM report cited in policy discussions found that 97% of AI security incidents involved systems with poor access controls; 63% of breached organizations had no AI governance policies.

The **SSGM framework** (Stability and Safety Governed Memory) proposes structured risk categories (temporal obsolescence, index bloat, retrieval latency, poisoning, privacy leakage) and corresponding governance mechanisms (decay, pruning, hierarchical indexing, write firewalls, ACLs), but acknowledges that real-world adoption is early.[22] The **Mnemonic Sovereignty** survey calls for giving users meaningful control over their persistent memories and emphasizes normative frameworks (rights, consent, revocability) beyond technical controls.[21]

### 6.4 Alignment, norms, and what “ought” to be remembered

Beyond technical risks, there are normative questions:

- **Which memories should be preserved vs forgotten?** Over-retention risks privacy and clutter; aggressive forgetting risks loss of accountability and important context.[21][22][26]  
- **Bias and entrenchment**: “Memory as Metabolism” analyzes how certain governance profiles can lead to **coherence-preserving drift**, where dominant interpretations become entrenched and legitimate revisions are suppressed.[14]  
- **Regulatory alignment**: policy documents and standards (e.g., NIST, ISO/IEC 42001, EU AI Act, OWASP Agentic Top 10) are starting to name **memory & context poisoning** and **agent governance** as explicit risk categories, but concrete technical requirements are still evolving.[10][17]

Context Cartography and related work treat these as **governance-layer design problems**, but the field lacks shared normative baselines for what “good” memory governance means in different application domains.

## 7. Key Insights

- **Memory governance is now a first-class discipline.** By 2026, both research and industry treat memory as a structured, governed subsystem with its own taxonomies, benchmarks, and best practices, rather than just “conversation history.”[1][3][5][26]

- **Context Cartography provides a unifying formalism.** Its \( V/G/B \) zones and cartographic operators offer a general vocabulary for reasoning about context movement and failure modes, into which OS-style tiers, graph memory, and enterprise context graphs can be mapped.[6][14][3][11]

- **Convergent design patterns are clear.** Most advanced systems converge on: (1) a small, structured visible field; (2) curated episodic and semantic stores; (3) explicit consolidation and forgetting; and (4) governance-aware retrieval and routing.[5][3][11][13][15]

- **Learned control is promising but immature.** Tiered memory with learned controllers and state-based consolidation (e.g., AgeMem, AtomMem, GAM, LIGHT) show strong benchmark gains, but are complex, data-hungry, and not yet widely deployed.[5][11][20][22]

- **Security and normative governance are emerging as central.** Memory poisoning, privacy leakage, and user control over persistent memory are now front-line concerns, with dedicated surveys (Mnemonic Sovereignty, SSGM) and policy frameworks (NIST, OWASP Agentic Top 10) starting to shape technical designs.[21][22][10]

---

## 8. Limitations and Gaps in the Current Research

- **Limited real-world longitudinal studies.** Most evaluations are on synthetic or bounded benchmarks; we lack multi-year, real-production studies of memory governance impacts on safety, cost, and user trust.[5][20][21]

- **Underdeveloped multi-agent governance.** Cross-agent memory sharing, consensus, and privacy-preserving knowledge transfer remain largely unsolved and under-evaluated.[5][6][22]

- **Fragmented evaluation standards.** While there are many benchmarks, there is no standardized, widely adopted suite for *governance-layer* evaluation (e.g., measuring drift control, fairness in forgetting, or resilience to poisoning) across frameworks.[5][20][21][14]

- **Normative ambiguity.** Technical frameworks acknowledge ethical and legal concerns, but concrete normative criteria for “good” memory governance (especially around rights, accountability, and entrenchment) are still being formulated.[21][22][26]

- **Tooling and accessibility.** Many advanced frameworks (e.g., learned controllers, formal verification in Lean) are research-prototype level; practical, developer-friendly tooling that encodes governance best practices is still emerging.[3][5][8][10]
